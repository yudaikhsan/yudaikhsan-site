## The brief

For a national telecom, the worst-case scenarios are not hypothetical: a network outage
that hits the entire country, a restructuring that touches hundreds of employees, a story
that turns hostile overnight. When I joined, each incident was handled as it arrived —
competently, but reactively.

We wanted the opposite: a system that made the *right* response the fast one.

## What I did

- Developed and stress-tested **crisis communication plans** with senior stakeholders, so
  roles, approvals, and holding lines were decided before an incident, not during one.
- Ran a **proactive monitoring system** for media coverage and public sentiment, so we
  could detect a brewing reputation risk early and intervene while it was still small.
- Built the **rapid response workflow** — one approved line, one team, one channel — for
  both external media and internal staff.
- Translated **complex technical incidents** into clear, human updates, with engineering
  and legal in the loop so nothing overstated or understated the situation.
- Treated every incident as a **campaign** afterwards: measured share of voice and
  sentiment against baseline, then folded the lessons back into the playbook.

## The outcome

- **Response time cut by roughly half**, because the plan existed before the pressure did.
- **Negative coverage reduced by around 40%** through early detection and timely, targeted
  intervention.
- Consistent messaging across traditional and digital channels during the incidents that
  mattered most — including nationwide service disruption and a 500-person restructuring.
- Leadership that could act on a briefing instead of a debate.

### The principle

A crisis plan you haven't rehearsed is a document, not a capability. The value isn't in
the binder — it's in the speed and calm that come from having practised.
