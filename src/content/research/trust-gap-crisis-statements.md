When a crisis breaks, the instinct inside most organisations is to withhold until the facts are complete. It feels responsible. To the audience, it reads as evasion.

This report examines that gap: the distance between what communicators think a statement must contain, and what a reader actually needs from it in the first hour.

## Method

I reviewed a set of corporate crisis statements issued within 24 hours of a public incident — covering service outages, safety events, and restructuring announcements — and assessed each against the questions audiences systematically ask, in order:

1. **Are you awake?** Has anyone noticed this is happening?
2. **Do you care?** Is there any human concern in the words?
3. **What should I do?** Is there an action for me, right now?
4. **What happened?** The facts, in order.
5. **What now?** The commitment and the timeline.

## What the pattern showed

The statements audiences rated most credible almost never led with facts. They led with **acknowledgement** — a plain sentence confirming the situation exists and matters. Completeness ranked *last*, not first.

Three recurring failures stood out:

- **The legal hedge.** Sentences engineered to admit nothing, which audiences read as guilt.
- **The passive voice.** "Mistakes were made" removes the actor and, with them, the accountability.
- **The premature cause.** Naming a cause before it's confirmed — the fastest way to a second crisis.

> Audiences don't grade a crisis statement on what it proves. They grade it on whether it sounds like a person.

## The five-line holding statement

Out of this came the template I now recommend for the first hour: five sentences, no more.

1. We are aware of X.
2. This matters to us, and here's why.
3. Here is what we are doing right now.
4. Here is what we don't yet know, honestly.
5. Here is when we will next update you.

It answers the only question live at that moment — *are you awake, and do you care?* — without guessing at facts that aren't confirmed.

## A caveat

This is qualitative work, drawn from a practitioner's reading rather than a controlled study. It describes patterns I keep seeing, not a statistically proven law. Treat it as a lens for reviewing your own statements, not a formula.
