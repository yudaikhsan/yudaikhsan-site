Most communication reporting stops at the easy number: how many articles mentioned us. That number feels good in a boardroom and tells you almost nothing. You can double your coverage and still lose the argument, because the one sentence you needed the audience to remember never appeared.

## The core idea

**Message pull-through** is the share of a campaign's key messages that actually survive the journey from your briefing document into published coverage. It's the difference between being *mentioned* and being *understood*.

I score every piece of coverage against three questions:

1. **Was the hero message present?** The one idea everything else serves.
2. **Was it attributed correctly?** The right spokesperson, the right framing.
3. **Was it framed the way we intended?** Not just quoted — *used*.

## The scoring sheet

| Level | What it looks like | Weight |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 — Mention only | Brand name appears, no message | 0 |
| 2 — Partial | A proof point, but not the hero idea | 0.5 |
| 3 — Full pull-through | Hero message present and correctly framed | 1 |

The weighted total, divided by total items, is your pull-through score. It's deliberately blunt — it has to be scored in minutes, by anyone on the team, without an argument.

## Why it beats share of voice

Share of voice tells you how loud you were relative to competitors. Pull-through tells you whether loudness was *pointed anywhere*. I've run both side by side, and the gap is instructive: campaigns that looked strong on volume often scored below 0.4 on pull-through — plenty of noise, not much signal.

> Volume measures effort. Pull-through measures transfer. Only one of them is a communication outcome.

## How I use it

- **Before launch:** agree the hero message in one sentence, so it's scorable later.
- **During:** review pull-through weekly and correct the messaging that isn't landing.
- **After:** report pull-through alongside volume in the post-mortem, so the conversation is about comprehension, not just column inches.

## Limitations

Pull-through is a proxy, not proof. It measures what journalists *published*, not what audiences *believed*. It should sit next to audience research, not replace it. But as a fast, repeatable, defensible number, it has earned its place in every campaign I run.
