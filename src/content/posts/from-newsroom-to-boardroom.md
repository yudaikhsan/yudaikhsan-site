My first job out of university was as a journalist at Kompas.com. I spent those months
learning a discipline that has never left me: get the facts first, get them right, and
get them out before the deadline. I still remember chasing a story on the Kalijodo
closures — one of the most-read pieces I've ever filed — and feeling the strange
satisfaction of a hard-won sentence.

I didn't know it then, but that newsroom instinct would become the most valuable tool in
a career I couldn't have predicted.

## The move to the other side

I crossed over into public relations, and eventually into agencies and in-house teams. On
the surface, the job flipped: instead of asking the questions, I was helping organisations
answer them. But the fundamentals never changed.

Journalists want the truth, fast, in a form they can use. So do regulators, customers,
and your own employees. The job is the same everywhere: **find the honest version of the
story and remove every obstacle between it and the person who needs to hear it.**

That's why I've never really felt "in opposition" to the media. I've sat on both sides of
the pitch, and I know exactly what makes a story run — and what gets it killed.

## What the newsroom gave me

- **A bias for clarity.** If a sentence can be misunderstood, it will be.
- **Respect for deadlines.** A perfect statement that arrives too late is a failure.
- **Comfort with pushback.** Ask the uncomfortable question early, while you can still
  act on the answer.

I went back to study too, completing a Master's in Communication Management, because I
wanted the theory behind the instincts — the frameworks that separate a lucky campaign
from a repeatable one, and measurement methods that let you defend your work in a
boardroom.

## What the boardroom taught me

Strategy is not the opposite of storytelling. It's what gives storytelling somewhere to
go. A great narrative with no objective is a campfire story. An objective with no
narrative is a spreadsheet. The craft is holding both.

Eleven years on, I still start every engagement the same way a reporter would: what's the
story, who needs to know, and why now?

> The tools changed. The discipline didn't.

If you're on the fence about crossing between media and communications — do it. The
people who understand both sides are the ones everyone wants in the room.
